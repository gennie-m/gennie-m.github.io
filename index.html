Featured work  1 / 5

Co-designing legal risk mitigation for medical AI tools

Challenge: While there's optimism around medical AI tools, companies and developers can shift legal exposure onto doctors and patients. Addressing these legal risks requires coordinating design across developers, lawyers, doctors, and patients.

Approach: Built an evaluation framework advancing user-centered evaluations of how people act on AI-provided information. Over 2 years, developed a novel co-design approach with 10 U.S. lawyers to predict and manage risks of medical AI tools, and analyzed 31 legal cases and reported harms to understand impacts on patient care.

Impact: A re-usable framework for evaluating whether an AI tool's outputs are actionable (published HCII 2026), an innovative approach to integrate lawyers' perspectives, and an accessible visual explainer of the method built in Adobe InDesign.

Featured work  2 / 5

Improving algorithms that explain AI agents to non-experts

Challenge: Reinforcement learning agents choose actions based on future rewards but discard the qualitative features of their training — making it nearly impossible to recover a user-understandable “why.” Pipelines calling LLMs, APIs, and other algorithms are likewise hard to interpret, intervene on, and modify, leaving users unable to change what an agent does.

Approach: Worked alongside the technical team developing Experiential Explanations, a technique that restores information about how a policy reflects its environment. I coordinated the analysis of a human evaluation study to inform future work, and helped teams apply World Models to explain RL agents and develop counterfactuals for AI pipelines.

Impact: Qualitative analysis surfaced which factors of experiential explanations were most useful, directly guiding further technical development, and informed human-factors benchmark use cases for Reverse World Models and Pipelines.

AI/ML in Education
Featured work  3 / 5

Concept Catalyst: Aligning AI-based tools with K-12 engineering teachers' perspectives

Challenge: Engineering teachers exercise a high degree of creativity and problem solving as they reflect on personal and professional experiences to create classroom content. While generative AI can help teachers meet demands such as grading or curriculum creation, it is critical to support their reflective practices, empowering them to shape their students' learning.

Approach: Our team was originally focused on AI-assisted grading. As I ran more teacher interviews, I kept hearing something different: the real burden was writing scaffolding questions, not grading. I raised that with the project leads, made the case to shift direction, and led the redesign.

Impact: 2 rounds of in-depth interviews with 8 engineering teachers. Prototyped and ran Wizard-of-Oz sessions with 10 more teachers — positive feedback on efficiency and boosted confidence using AI in their classrooms. Shipped a working ChatGPT-based tool.

AI curriculum generator for Borders:none (Zagreb, Croatia)

Challenge: Borders:none, a non-profit supporting migrants and refugees, broadly described wanting some technology to support their volunteer teachers. The challenge was working with stakeholders to identify the goal and design so it would truly serve volunteers' needs rather than add complexity to their work.

Approach: Built a generative-AI pipeline that converts YouTube transcripts into ready-to-use lesson content. Wrote and won a competitive NSF INTERN grant ($18k total). Impact: A tool designed around volunteers' actual time constraints and workflow — grounded in field research instead of assumptions.

Chameleon Clippers: Fine motor skill feedback for remote K-2 art education

Challenge: Museum outreach greatly enriches K-2 art education, but little research had explored bringing that content into fully remote classrooms. A needs assessment (22 surveys, 4 interviews) surfaced a specific, unmet gap: young students had no way to get feedback on the fine motor skills, like scissor control, that art-making depends on.

Approach: Built a typology of existing remote art platforms and evaluated it against educator-defined requirements, then designed and hand-built a prototype interactive pair of scissors — embedding sensors and a microcontroller into a child-friendly chameleon design — to sense and respond to a child's cutting technique in real time.

Impact: Preliminary user testing with 12 children (ages 4–7) showed the prototype could surface fine motor feedback during real cutting tasks. The accompanying paper won Best Technical Paper at CSCL 2022.
